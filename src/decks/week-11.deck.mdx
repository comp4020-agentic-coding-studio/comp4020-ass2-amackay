## Holophrasm (2016)

Daniel Whalen's system, as its record describes it, explores partial proof
trees with "a neural-network-augmented bandit algorithm" and "proves 14% of
its test theorems from Metamath's set.mm module".

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1608.02644)

```notes
The first of the line. Fourteen percent of its own test theorems, on the
database you have verified every week since week 1.
```

## GPT-f (2020)

Polu and Sutskever present "an automated prover and proof assistant, GPT-f,
for the Metamath formalization language".

It "found new short proofs that were accepted into the main Metamath library".

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2009.03393)

```notes
A language model, pointed at set.mm. The second sentence is the one the next
slide is about.
```

## MetaGen (2020)

Wang and Deng train "a neural generator that automatically synthesizes
theorems and proofs for the purpose of training a theorem prover", with
experiments in Metamath.

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2002.07019)

```notes
Same year as GPT-f, a different move: instead of training a prover on set.mm,
generate more theorems to train it on.
```

---

## HyperTree Proof Search (2022)

Lample and colleagues describe a search "inspired by the recent success of
AlphaZero".

Their record reports, on a held-out set of Metamath theorems:

- 65.4%, against "56.5% by GPT-f"
- 82.6% with online training

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2205.11491)

```notes
These are the numbers later work measures against, and they are in
this week's reading. Held-out set: the theorems kept back from training.
```

---

## REFACTOR (2024)

Zhou and colleagues train a network to extract reusable theorems from proofs.

Applied "to the existing Metamath library, REFACTOR extracted 16 new
theorems".

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2402.17032)

```notes
Not proving, extracting: finding a lemma inside existing proofs and pulling it
out. Sixteen, by its own count.
```

---

## Grammar-compressed proof terms (2025)

Wernhard and Zombori take Metamath as their "source of human-structured
proofs" and treat a knowledge base as "a grammar that compresses a set of
gigantic proof trees".

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2505.12305)

```notes
The most recent, and the one furthest from proof search: the database itself,
read as a compression scheme.
```

## The name to know

"MetaMath: Bootstrap Your Own Mathematical Questions for Large Language
Models", and its dataset MetaMathQA.

Its record describes a language model fine-tuned on rewritten mathematical
questions, scored on GSM8K and MATH.

Nothing in that record concerns the Metamath language.

[arXiv record](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2309.12284)

```notes
Capital M, machine-learning context, benchmark names you have not seen in this
course: that is the tell.
```

## Before Wednesday's lab

- Read the header of
  [pmproofs.txt](https://us.metamath.org/mmsolitaire/pmproofs.txt)
- Learn its notation: `1`, `2`, `3` are the axioms, `D` is ax-mp
- Pick a theorem whose proof is at most nine characters; bring its line
- Bring mmj2 and [mmverify.py](https://us.metamath.org/downloads/mmverify.py)
